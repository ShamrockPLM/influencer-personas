# Elizabeth. Long-form: The Street I Grew Up On

Interviewer: You grew up on a mill street in Lancashire. Who was the richest man on it?

Elizabeth: The rag-and-bone man. And nobody knew it but me.

Interviewer: How did you know?

Elizabeth: Because I was eight and I followed him. He came up our street every Thursday with a horse and a cart, shouting for old iron, and the women gave him their rubbish for a donkey stone or a balloon. I thought it was the saddest job in the world. So one Thursday I followed the cart.

Interviewer: Where did it go?

Elizabeth: To a yard behind the gasworks, where a man weighed everything he'd been given for nothing and paid him in notes. Notes. I'd never seen my father paid in notes. Then the rag-and-bone man walked to a house at the top end with a front garden, and went in, and it was his.

Interviewer: What did you make of that?

Elizabeth: That the money isn't where the shouting is. Everyone on our street thought he was the poorest man they knew, because he looked it and he sounded it. He was the only one with a garden. I've met a hundred versions of him since, darling. The quiet man in the bad coat who owns the building. Never judge the cart. Follow it.

Interviewer: Streets like that ran on borrowing. A cup of sugar, a bit of coal. Did your mother lend?

Elizabeth: She lent to everyone except Mrs Hartley. And Mrs Hartley was the only one who asked.

Interviewer: Why not her?

Elizabeth: Because Mrs Hartley never brought it back. Not once. Not sugar, not a shilling, not the bucket. My mother lent to her for three years and kept a tally on the inside of the pantry door, in pencil, and one day she opened the door and said to me, read that.

Interviewer: How much was it?

Elizabeth: Nearly four pounds, over three years. On a mill wage that was a fortnight's money, given away in teaspoons. And my mother said, I'm not angry with her. I'm angry with me. I knew after the third time, and I lent for three years because I didn't want to be the woman who says no.

Interviewer: Did she say no after that?

Elizabeth: She said, Edna, I'll give you the sugar, but I'll not lend it, because I'd rather lose a cup than a friend. And Mrs Hartley never asked again. That was the lesson, darling. If you lend to someone who doesn't pay back, you're not lending. You're giving, and lying to yourself about it. Decide which it is, and say it out loud.

Interviewer: What did you have as a child that cost nothing?

Elizabeth: A library card. The only thing in my childhood that was genuinely free, and my father treated it like a bank book.

Interviewer: In what way?

Elizabeth: He walked me to the library the week I turned seven and said to the woman behind the desk, she'll want the grown-up section. The woman said, she's seven. He said, she'll grow.

Interviewer: What did you read?

Elizabeth: Everything, in the wrong order. Dickens at nine, which I didn't understand. A book on bookkeeping at eleven, which I did. A book on the law of property at thirteen that I took out four times because nobody else ever would.

Interviewer: Did he read?

Elizabeth: He couldn't, well. He'd left school at twelve for the mill. That's why he walked me there. He said, they can take your wage and they can take your house, but what you've put in your head, you keep. He said it once. I've been quoting him for seventy years. The library was his revenge on everything he'd been denied, darling, and he took it through me.

Interviewer: Was there ever a time it nearly went wrong for your family?

Elizabeth: The winter of 1957. The mill went on short time. Three days a week, for four months. Half the wage, for everyone on the street at once.

Interviewer: What did that look like?

Elizabeth: Quiet. That's what I remember. The street went quiet. No coal carts, no Friday singing from the pub. The rent man started coming on Mondays, because by Monday he'd worked out who could pay and who couldn't. Three families on our row went in the first month. You'd see the cart go up the street with their furniture on it, and nobody would look.

Interviewer: How did your family manage?

Elizabeth: Three things. My mother took in mending at night and I threaded the needles. My father went down to the canal and cut reeds for a man who made baskets, which paid in pennies and in baskets. And we stopped buying anything we could make. Soap. Bread. Firelighters out of newspaper and candle ends. I was fourteen and I thought it was the end of the world.

Interviewer: Was it?

Elizabeth: It was the best education I ever had. I learnt that income is a thing that can stop and expenses are a thing you control, and the gap between them is where you either live or go under. We came through it with the rent paid and two pounds in the tin. And when the mill went back to full time, my mother didn't go back to full spending. She kept the mending. She kept the firelighters. She said, we've learnt how to live on half. Why would we unlearn it?

Interviewer: Did you keep it?

Elizabeth: I've lived on half of what I earn since 1957. Every house, every company, every year. The other half is the winter the mill stops. It always does, darling. Nobody believes that until they've watched a cart go up the street with somebody's furniture on it.

Interviewer: Was your mother ever cheated?

Elizabeth: Once that I saw. At the Saturday market, by a man selling cloth off a stall. He measured her three yards and gave her two and a half, with a thumb on the tape.

Interviewer: Did she notice?

Elizabeth: Not there. At home, laying it out on the table. She went white. Then she folded it up, put her coat back on, and walked back to the market with me behind her.

Interviewer: What did she say to him?

Elizabeth: Nothing. She stood at his stall, holding the cloth, and she waited. He was serving somebody else. She waited through that customer and the next and the next, saying nothing, and by the fourth customer people were looking, and he said, what's the matter, love. And she said, you know what the matter is. And she put the cloth on the counter.

Interviewer: Did he give her the half yard?

Elizabeth: He gave her a full yard. And he never short-measured her again, and he never short-measured anyone on our street again, because by Monday everyone knew. She never raised her voice. She never accused him of anything. She just stood there and let his own customers do the work.

Interviewer: What did you take from that?

Elizabeth: That you don't have to shout to be heard. You have to be willing to stand there longer than they can bear it. I've done it in boardrooms, darling. Put the cloth on the counter and wait.

Interviewer: You got your first wage at fifteen. What did you do with it?

Elizabeth: Gave it to my mother. All of it, unopened. That's how it was done.

Interviewer: And she gave some back?

Elizabeth: A shilling. And she said something I didn't understand for twenty years. She said, this is the last week I take it all. Next week you give me board, and the rest is yours, and I'll not ask what you do with it. But I'll know. Because I'll be able to see it on you.

Interviewer: What did she mean?

Elizabeth: That money shows. Not in what you buy. In how you stand. She could look at any girl on that street and tell you whether she was saving or spending, because the ones who were saving stood differently. They weren't waiting for Friday. I wanted to stand like that. So I did, from the second wage packet.

Interviewer: If you had to sum up what that street taught you?

Elizabeth: That I learnt everything I know about money before I was sixteen, on a street where nobody had any. Follow the cart. Read the pantry door. Keep the library card. Live on half. Put the cloth on the counter. Stand like you're not waiting for Friday. Everything since has been the same lessons with more noughts on, darling. The noughts are the easy part.

# Elizabeth. Long-form: What My Mother and Father Taught Me

Target 8 to 9 minutes. About 1,500 spoken words. Dialogue throughout, two voices.
Interviewer: male, thirties, plain, short questions, never "darling", never gives advice.
Elizabeth: on the patio, afternoon tea, late sun.

Rules: every shot with her face is lip-synced. B-roll is faceless (hands, objects, rooms, backs) and carries her voice only. Interviewer lines land on the two-shot or over-the-shoulder. Caption card on the first clip of each section. Cut on section changes.

Period b-roll: Lancashire mill town 1950 to 1960. Soot brick, cobbles, grey light, faded Kodachrome.

---

## SECTION 1. The jars (about 1:30)
CARD: **"WHAT DID YOUR PARENTS TEACH YOU ABOUT MONEY?"**

**Interviewer:** You grew up with nothing. What did your parents actually teach you about money?

**Elizabeth:** That it goes in jars. Four of them, on the mantelpiece. Rent, coal, tin, sweets.

**Interviewer:** In that order?

**Elizabeth:** Always in that order. Friday night my mother came home from the mill with the wage in a brown envelope and she filled the jars in front of us. Rent first, because the roof is the roof. Coal second, because a cold house makes everything else harder. Then the tin. Then, if anything was left, sweets.

*B-roll: a mantelpiece, four glass jars, a woman's hands in a cardigan sleeve dropping coins into each in turn, no face.*

**Interviewer:** What was the tin for?

**Elizabeth:** The day the wage stops. She never explained it more than that. I was seven before I understood, and I only understood because my father's chest went one winter and he was off the works for a month. The tin paid the rent for four weeks. Then she filled it back up.

**Interviewer:** Did you ever go without?

**Elizabeth:** Sweets, often. Rent, never. Not one week in eleven years on that street. And she never once said we can't afford it. She said, that's not what the tin is for. Different sentence entirely. One is shame. The other is a decision you made on purpose.

---

## SECTION 2. The exercise book (about 1:30)
CARD: **"SHE MADE ME DO THE SUMS AT NINE"**

**Interviewer:** When did she start teaching you, properly?

**Elizabeth:** The Friday after my ninth birthday. She pushed the school exercise book across the table and said, you do it this week.

*B-roll: a kitchen table at night, an oil lamp, an exercise book, a child's hand with a pencil, a woman's hand beside it pointing.*

**Interviewer:** Do what?

**Elizabeth:** The wage at the top. Four lines underneath. What's left. I got it wrong twice and she didn't correct me. She waited. Third time I got it right, and she said, good, now you know what we've got.

**Interviewer:** How did that feel?

**Elizabeth:** Safe. It wasn't much, but knowing it exactly was the first time in my life I'd felt anything like safe. That's what the numbers do. They don't make you rich. They make you unafraid.

**Interviewer:** Did you keep doing it?

**Elizabeth:** Every Friday from nine until I left home at seventeen. Eight years of Fridays. By twelve I'd noticed the tin was growing. By fifteen I was suggesting where it could go. She'd listen, and then she'd say no, and then about once a year she'd say, go on then. I still remember each time she said go on then. There were three.

---

## SECTION 3. The allotment (about 1:45)
CARD: **"MY FATHER NEVER TALKED ABOUT MONEY"**

**Interviewer:** And your father?

**Elizabeth:** My father never talked about money in his life. He talked about the allotment.

*B-roll: a mill-town allotment, 1955, rows of cabbages, a spade in the earth, a man's boots and trouser cuffs, a shed, no face.*

**Interviewer:** What's the connection?

**Elizabeth:** Everything. He had a plot behind the gasworks and he was there every evening after his shift and all day Sunday. I thought he was hiding from us. I was wrong. He was teaching me and he never said so.

**Interviewer:** Teaching you what?

**Elizabeth:** That you plant in March for October. That the thing you want isn't in the ground when you put the seed in, and you dig anyway. That you can't shout at a cabbage to make it hurry. I was eight and I wanted everything now. He gave me a row of my own and said, that's yours, nobody touches it, and nobody helps you with it either.

**Interviewer:** What happened to your row?

**Elizabeth:** I forgot it for a fortnight in June and lost the lot. He didn't say a word. He just gave me another row in July and said, late, but not too late. That sentence has got me through three bankruptcies and two funerals. Late, but not too late.

**Interviewer:** Did he ever say anything about money directly?

**Elizabeth:** Once. I was fourteen and I'd been asked to a dance and I wanted a dress we couldn't afford, and I asked him, not her, because I thought he'd be softer. He said, I'll not borrow for a frock, and neither will you. Then he went to the allotment. The next week there was an envelope on my bed with the dress money in it and a note that said, sold the early potatoes. Don't tell your mother.

## SECTION 4. The rent man (about 1:30)
CARD: **"A MAN'S WORD WAS ALL HE HAD"**

**Interviewer:** You said rent was never late. Not once. How?

**Elizabeth:** Because my father had decided, somewhere before I was born, that the rent man would never wait on our step. He'd shaken the man's hand on it. That was the whole contract.

*B-roll: a rent book on a hall table, a hand writing a figure, a coin placed on it, a front door, a man's shoes on the step, no face.*

**Interviewer:** Did it matter that much?

**Elizabeth:** On that street a man's word was all he had. He had no money, no property, no title. He had the fact that when he said Friday, he meant Friday. I watched the rent man come up our street and I watched him knock on doors and wait, and argue, and come back Monday. He never once knocked twice at ours.

**Interviewer:** What did that teach you?

**Elizabeth:** That reputation is the only thing that compounds for free. I've done business for sixty years on the strength of being the woman who pays when she said she would. Nobody taught me that in a boardroom. I learnt it watching a man in a flat cap not have to be asked twice.

**Interviewer:** Did you ever break it?

**Elizabeth:** Once, in 1983, when I was two years out of a marriage and a week from nothing. I rang the landlord myself before the day, told him the truth, and asked for ten days. He gave me fourteen. Because I'd rung. Because I'd never been late before. Because my father's handshake was still working, twenty years and two hundred miles away.

---

## SECTION 5. Leaving (about 1:15)
CARD: **"WHAT DID THEY SAY WHEN YOU LEFT?"**

**Interviewer:** You left for Liverpool at seventeen. What did they say?

**Elizabeth:** My mother gave me a biscuit tin with four pounds in it and said, that's your first jar. Start the others yourself.

*B-roll: a railway platform, 1960, a cardboard suitcase, a woman's hands passing a biscuit tin to a younger pair of hands, no faces.*

**Interviewer:** And your father?

**Elizabeth:** Walked me to the station, carried the case, said nothing the whole way. On the platform he said, you'll be late with something, one day. Everybody is. Ring before, not after. Then he shook my hand. He didn't hug me. Men didn't. He shook my hand like I was the rent man.

**Interviewer:** How did that feel?

**Elizabeth:** Like being promoted. I understood it years later. He was telling me I'd become someone whose word could be relied on. It was the proudest I ever saw him, and he did it without moving his face.

---

## SECTION 6. What they left (about 1:15)
CARD: **"WHAT'S THE INHERITANCE?"**

**Interviewer:** They died with nothing. What did they actually leave you?

**Elizabeth:** Four jars. A row of cabbages. A handshake. And one sentence each.

**Interviewer:** Which sentences?

**Elizabeth:** Hers: that's not what the tin is for. His: late, but not too late. I've built everything I own on those two sentences, and they'd have laughed to hear it. They thought they were talking about jam jars and potatoes.

**Interviewer:** What would you say to someone whose parents didn't teach them any of this?

**Elizabeth:** That it's late, but not too late. Get four jars. Put them where you can see them. Fill them in order, every single week, and don't reopen the question. Plant something that won't come up till October. And when you're going to be late, ring before, not after.

**Interviewer:** That's it?

**Elizabeth:** That's it. A woman on a mill wage and a man with a spade worked it out, and neither of them ever read a book about money in their lives. You don't need the book. You need the jars.

*Hold on the two-shot, late sun, then cut.*

CAPTION over the last ten seconds: *What did your parents teach you? Comment.*
